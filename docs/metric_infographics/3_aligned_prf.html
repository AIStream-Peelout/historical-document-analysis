Aligned precision, recall and F1 · completeness versus accuracy

Read a little, or read it all?

Character error rate divides by the length of the reference, so a system is rewarded for attempting less: say almost nothing and most of the page simply never enters the sum. Precision and recall over an alignment split that single number in two — how much of what was written is real, and how much of the page was recovered.

1

Align the output to the page

Both texts are already reduced to visible ink. A sequence alignment then walks the two strings and finds the longest runs of characters that match in the same order — no re-ordering, no jumping back. Everything outside those runs is either invented by the model or missed on the page.

3

Precision: how much of what it wrote is real

Divide the matched characters by the length of the output. The eleven characters of invented blessing are pure cost here — they are text the model asserted and the page does not carry.

0.7028 matched ÷ 40 output characters
4

Recall: how much of the page it recovered

Same numerator, divided by the length of the reference. The invented blessing is invisible to this score; only the one misread letter is missing from the page's ink.

0.9728 matched ÷ 29 reference characters

The two disagree sharply on this output, and that disagreement is the finding: the model read the page almost completely, then kept writing.

5

F1 balances them

0.812PR ÷ (P + R), with P = 0.70 and R = 0.97

The harmonic mean punishes a system that wins one side by abandoning the other. Compare our verbose reader with a cautious one that transcribes only the first two words and stops — every character it writes is correct.

6

Order matters here

Take the same output and swap its two halves — the same characters, the same words, read in a different sequence.

Output, halves swappedמעליא ויכנו ויצליחו שלום וברכה בנחשא טבא

To the order-blind five-letter window score of page 1 nothing has happened: every window survives the move, and the score stays at 0.53. The alignment cannot follow. It is allowed to match only runs that appear in the same order on both sides, so it must settle for the longer half and abandon the rest — recall and F1 both fall, while the ink read is identical.

This is why the paper reports both numbers. Five-gram precision is invariant to reading order; aligned F1 is not. The gap between them on a given system is a measurement of the reading-order and layout burden — how much of its score it loses not to misreading letters, but to serialising a page in an order the editor did not use.

Why report precision and recall separately

Three things one number hides

41outputs with precision ≥ 0.85 but recall ≤ 0.30

Accurate fragments of a page: what they wrote is real, but they stopped. Their CER sits around 0.99, indistinguishable from a blank answer. GPT-5.6 Sol on T-S 10J12/4 scores precision 0.94, recall 0.01, CER 0.99 — three numbers, only two of which are informative.

0.730 vs 0.678median aligned F1, raw Kraken lines versus Kraken after LLM reconstruction

The reconstruction step costs order on linear pages. Five-gram precision barely moves across the same pair (0.476 versus 0.474), so almost none of the loss is misread ink: it is reading order alone. The fine-tuned VLMs that followed the paper close the gap from the other side: HebVL-2.1 reaches a median F1 of 0.880 with 5-gram precision 0.661, above Kraken on both.

33outputs with 5-gram precision ≥ 0.25 but aligned precision ≤ 0.5

Fifteen of them are Kraken. The ink was read correctly, in an order the reference does not use. Alignment punishes these outputs; the order-blind score forgives them. Neither verdict is wrong, which is the argument for keeping both.

What it does not see

An order-aware score, with an order-aware score's blind spots

It counts characters, not confusions. A near-miss letter breaks a matching block but costs only the one character, so alignment is far kinder to systematic letter confusions than the five-gram score, where a single wrong letter takes out five windows. The two metrics disagree on 139 outputs where every window broke but half the characters still aligned.
On multi-layer pages the reference order is an editor's choice. Where marginalia, over-writing or an under-text have to be linearised, Tier B F1 measures agreement with that choice, not reading ability. A different but defensible ordering scores as a failure.
The medians are over all outputs, abstentions included. A refusal scores 0 and is averaged in with genuine attempts, so the table above mixes behaviour with accuracy. That is why the paper always pairs it with the behaviour table rather than reading the medians alone.
Precision alone is never the arbiter. Of the 139 disagreements above, 51 are stubs under 15 % of the reference length: high precision, recall near 0.03. High precision on a fragment of a fragment is not a good transcription, and only the recall term says so.
