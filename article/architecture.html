Qwen3-VL-8B is two stacks joined by a projector. A 27-block vision transformer turns 16×16 image patches into 1,152-dimensional tokens; a merger folds each 2×2 group of them into one 4,096-dimensional token; a 36-layer language model consumes those alongside the text embeddings. Three extra taps — the deepstack mergers — carry features out of vision blocks 8, 16 and 24 and inject them into the language stack directly.

Our LoRA config wrapped 360 of those projections. The interesting part is not the 360; it is which of them the gradient actually reached.

The LoRA itself is unremarkable, which is part of the point — nothing in the configuration hints at the failure. Rank 16, alpha 16, dropout 0, no bias, no modules saved in full. Alpha equal to rank means the scale factor is exactly 1.0, so the adapter's contribution is B·A with no amplification.

Two details in that chart are worth pausing on. The vision updates in v1.8b are an order of magnitude smaller than the language updates — 0.17 against 3.79 on average — which is expected: the vision tower is a seventh the width and was trained at the same learning rate for the same number of steps. Small is fine. Zero is not.

And the language curve is essentially unchanged across all four generations. Whatever v1.8b gained on manuscripts, it did not come from training the language half differently. It came from the 7.7 million parameters in the other tower finally moving.

How far the language half got on its own

The uncomfortable part of the vision bug is that it took so long to notice, and the reason it took so long is that the language-only models were good. v1.7 — 108 dead vision tensors and all — posted the best gemara score in the whole benchmark, ahead of Gemini Pro and every frontier model we tested. On printed Talmud, a frozen vision tower was not the bottleneck at all.

Which says something about where the difficulty actually sat. Qwen3-VL's stock vision tower could already resolve Vilna type perfectly well; what it could not do was turn those features into Hebrew text, because the language model had never been asked to. Teaching the language half to decode was enough to close the entire gap on print.

A Vilna page carries three separate text bodies, and only one of them is in the square Hebrew type most people picture. The gemara sits in the centre in square script; Rashi's commentary and the tosafot flank it in Rashi script — a semi-cursive Sephardic face with genuinely different letterforms. Since all three are on the same scan, they make an unusually clean natural experiment.

How these were produced. Architecture dimensions come from the model's own config.json. Module inventory and parameter counts come from the base checkpoint's safetensors headers. Every "trained / not trained" claim is read from the shipped adapters themselves — the LoRA tensors were loaded and checked element-wise, not inferred from the training config, which reports vision as a target in all four generations.

Caveat. The four checkpoints shown were produced by the Unsloth/PEFT path on Colab, which is what shipped. The repo also carries an MLX training path for local runs whose YAML uses alpha 32 rather than 16; those configs did not produce these adapters.
